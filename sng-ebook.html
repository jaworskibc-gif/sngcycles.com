Chapter One

The Problem

Every motorcycle ever made was built for someone 5'10". Then the electric revolution came — and the problem got worse.

I'm 6'7". I've been riding since I was a kid. KTMs mostly — the 690 Duke, the 450 SXF, the 525 SM. I know what a serious motorcycle feels like. I know what it's like to throw a leg over something that was actually designed to perform, not just look like it performs.

And for my entire life, every single one of those bikes was built for someone who isn't me.

The seat height on a KTM 450 SXF — one of the best supermoto platforms ever built — is 37.6 inches. You're already cramped if you're anywhere over 6'2". For me, at 6'7" and 209 pounds, riding a stock supermoto means knees up near the handlebars, body folded like a jackknife, fighting geometry that was never designed to let a tall rider be comfortable, let alone fast. The motorcycle industry has been building bikes for the average rider for seventy-plus years. The average rider, apparently, is 5'10".

I dealt with it because the options were either adapt to what existed or stop riding. I adapted. I learned how to be quick on machines that weren't built for my body. But I never stopped noticing the gap.

"Every motorcycle ever made was built for someone 5'10". The electric revolution came. And instead of fixing that problem, the industry just electrified it."

Bryan Jaworski, Founder

Then electric supermotos started showing up. I paid attention. I watched the Stark VARG come out and generate huge buzz. I looked at what Cake and KTM Freeride and the other players were doing. The performance was getting real. Torque delivery on electric motors is honestly better suited to supermoto than almost any other application — instant, precise, controllable. The technology was finally there.

But nobody had built the right bike.

The Stark VARG is built on the same 37.4-inch seat height as a conventional supermoto. Lighter than a gas bike — 250 pounds — which is genuinely impressive. But still built for the 5'10" rider. Still the same geometry the industry has been running for decades. The electric revolution came to supermotos and brought exactly the same ergonomic blind spot it inherited from ICE.

And there was a second problem. The serious rider — the person who bought a Ducati Hypermotard or a KTM 890 SMR, the collector, the track-day buyer, the person who understands what premium suspension and proper geometry and hand-selected components actually means — had nowhere to go. The electric supermoto market was either race-only enduros, underpowered commuters, or kit bikes with bargain-bin controllers and forks pulled from whatever donor platform happened to be cheap.

Nobody was building an electric supermoto for the buyer who knew what they were buying. Nobody was building one for the tall rider. Nobody was building one with the kind of component selection that makes a motorcycle a real motorcycle instead of a conversion project.

The Gap in the Market

The serious electric supermoto buyer — tall, experienced, collector-minded — had exactly zero options. The market served the mass average and called it done.

SNG was built because that buyer existed and nobody was building their motorcycle.

I spent a long time waiting for someone else to fix this. I followed every release announcement. I read every spec sheet. I watched the community forums and the review channels and the launch events. And the bike I was waiting for never came.

So I stopped waiting.

The decision to build my own motorcycle wasn't a business plan hatched on a whiteboard. It was the natural conclusion of a guy who rides, who understands components, who had the skills and the frustration and the refusal to accept that the right machine just wasn't going to exist. I had the background. I had the data from years on KTMs. I had the specific knowledge of what I needed. And I had the recognition that if the tall rider's electric supermoto was ever going to exist, I was going to have to build it myself.

That's how SNG Cycles started. Not with a pitch deck. Not with a market research report. With a problem that nobody else was solving and a decision to stop waiting for someone who never showed up.

Build One — What a Big Rider Actually Needs

The suspension was too light. The power made it squirrelly. Good — now I know.

Build One worked. It rode. But the suspension was spec'd for an average-sized rider, and when real power hit it with 209 pounds on top, it became unpredictable — too much movement, not enough control. So the swingarm got extended. And once the swingarm moved, the geometry shifted. And once the geometry shifted, the wheels became part of the equation. One solution led to the next problem, which led to the next solution. That chain of cause and effect is how you end up with a machine that's 100 pounds lighter than the Stark VARG while being taller and built for a bigger rider. Build One didn't tell me what I wanted. It told me exactly what the machine needed.

Build Two — Patience and the Blueprint for Big and Light

A lot of that build was waiting. Waiting for the right parts. That was the lesson.

Build Two taught patience. Not the kind of patience where you sit still — the kind where you stop and wait for what's right instead of grabbing what's available. A significant part of those 14 months wasn't wrench time. It was waiting. Waiting for the right suspension components. Waiting for the CNC work. Refusing to compromise on parts because the wrong part would mean doing it again. Build Two also proved something that shouldn't be possible: you can build bigger — taller geometry, more stature, designed for a larger rider — and end up lighter. Every deliberate decision shaved weight. Every "good enough" shortcut that didn't get taken kept the number going down. That blueprint is what made Unit 001 possible.

Chapter Three

The Engineering

Every decision on the machine explained — from the forks to the battery enclosure to the chain nobody else runs.

A motorcycle is a system. Every component affects every other component. The decisions on an SNG machine aren't independent choices — they're an integrated stack that was built from the rider backward: a 6'7", 209-pound experienced rider who knows what a proper motorcycle feels like and will immediately notice if any part of the system is wrong.

Here's how the machine was built, and why.

01

Gas Tank Battery Enclosure

The community standard for electric supermoto battery housing is a 3D-printed plastic block bolted to the frame. Bryan looked at that solution and recognized the answer was already sitting in every motorcycle salvage yard in the country. He sourced a Husqvarna gas tank shell, cut the bottom off, and retained the OEM top mounting points to attach it directly to the frame. He then cut the center out of the stock Sur-Ron battery door, mixed shop clay, water, and baking soda into a working plaster, formed it over the old door, and mounted the tank shell on top — creating a seamless, factory-quality transition between the two pieces. The battery itself installs at a specific angle, which locks it in place by geometry alone — no latches, no straps, no extra hardware. The result looks like it was engineered by the OEM. There are zero documented examples of this approach anywhere in the electric motorcycle community.

02

Clamshell Hood-Release System (Patent Pending)

Once the tank enclosure is in place, you need a way to access the battery and electronics without fighting the bodywork. Bryan built a custom upper subframe section with swivel fairing spreaders — the same principle as a car hood release. Pull the mechanism, the rear plastics flare open and stay open, giving full access to the battery, the controller, and the wiring without removing a single bolt. Close it and everything snaps back flush. No tools. No disassembly. No production motorcycle on earth has anything like this. It is currently patent-pending.

03

WP XACT 4 Forks

The electric supermoto community defaults to Sirius or KKE forks. They're popular because they're available and reasonably priced. Bryan chose WP XACT 4. The reason isn't arbitrary brand loyalty — he had data from three KTMs' worth of riding on WP suspension. He knew exactly how they behaved, exactly where they were in the performance window, and exactly how they'd feel under his weight and riding style at the seat height he was targeting. WP XACT 4 also offered more travel and came in lighter and a thousand dollars cheaper than comparable alternatives once the full specification was compared. The community consensus wasn't wrong for the community. For this machine, the data said WP.

04

160mm Extended CNC Swingarm — 7075 T6 Aluminum

The swingarm extension was the decision that killed Prototype 2. The geometry has to be right or the bike doesn't work — full stop. The final spec is a 160mm extended swingarm machined from 7075 T6 aluminum, which is aerospace-grade — the same alloy series used in aircraft structural components and high-stress mechanical assemblies. The extension is built from 100mm and 60mm extenders stacked to achieve the final geometry. The result is the handling characteristic and weight distribution that makes a 202-pound supermoto work for a 6'7" rider at speed. The next evolution: a single billet custom swingarm that eliminates the extender stack entirely.

05

27kW Sotion Mid-Drive + BAC8000 Controller

The power system is the heart of the machine. The 27kW Sotion mid-drive delivers torque in the way that makes electric supermotos genuinely better than their gas counterparts for this application — instant, linear, controllable from the grip. The BAC8000 controller manages that delivery with the thermal management and integration quality the system requires. This is the combination that was missing on Prototype 1 and that made everything else on Prototype 3 work properly. The 72V system architecture was chosen for the voltage window that balances performance, battery architecture, and component availability at the production quality level SNG demands.

06

520 Primary Chain

Most electric supermoto conversions run a belt or a smaller chain because they're lighter and simpler. SNG runs a 520 primary chain — the chain pitch used on serious performance motorcycles. It's heavier on paper. The reasoning: a 520 chain handles the torque delivery of the Sotion mid-drive with a larger safety margin, handles the abuse of serious riding without the failure modes that show up in lightweight chain systems under sustained hard use, and integrates with the drivetrain as a complete system rather than as a compromise. On a $21,000 motorcycle, the drivetrain doesn't compromise.

07

UFO ABS Plastics — Hand-Formed On Site

UFO Plast is an Italian manufacturer that supplies bodywork to factory motocross and supermoto race programs. The ABS panels on an SNG machine start as premium Italian motorsport-grade material and are heat-formed and shaped on site — literally by hand, with a heat gun and a razor blade, until the fitment and lines are right. This is not a bolt-on cosmetic exercise. The hand-forming process, combined with the gas tank enclosure mounting system and the clamshell rear section, creates bodywork integration that doesn't exist anywhere else. The Holochrome edition adds a iridescent color-shifting wrap over this base that changes from blue to green to gold to purple depending on the angle of light. It cannot be accurately photographed. It has to be seen in person.

08

Lewis Brakes EP8+ & Warp 9 Wheels

Braking and rolling stock are where the difference between a motorcycle and a conversion project is most viscerally felt. Lewis Brakes EP8+ hydraulic disc brakes were selected for their performance window and integration with the SNG weight and power profile. Warp 9 wheels are the standard for serious off-road and supermoto application — proven, lightweight, and built to take the kind of loading that occurs on a performance machine used the way a supermoto is supposed to be used. These are not value-engineered components. They are the right components for the application.

Weight: The Engineering Result

Every component decision on an SNG machine is made with the weight target in mind. 202 pounds is the result. That is lighter than the Stark VARG SM at 275 pounds. Lighter than any comparable electric supermoto on the market. Not by trimming corners — by making better decisions at every system level.

Chapter Four

The Philosophy

Shark Not Goldfish. What it means, where it came from, and why it's tattooed on both hands.

Shark
Not
Goldfish.

The name is the philosophy. The philosophy is the brand.

A goldfish has a short memory and a smaller world. When the tank changes — when the water gets turbid or the environment shifts — the goldfish panics. It doesn't have the context or the capability to adapt. It just reacts, or it doesn't.

A shark has been in the ocean for four hundred and fifty million years. The ocean has changed — the temperature, the currents, the chemistry, the species that surround it. The shark adapted and thrived. It doesn't need the conditions to be stable. It thrives precisely because the conditions change. The waves don't threaten it. The waves are where it operates.

The electric revolution in motorcycles is a wave. Most of the industry is behaving like a goldfish — waiting to see how it shakes out, building half-measures, hedging. The legacy manufacturers spent decades building the internal combustion engine to a level of refinement that is genuinely impressive, and now they're watching that refinement become obsolete faster than their product cycles can adapt. That's the goldfish problem. The water changed and they're trying to figure out how to survive in the new conditions.

"The electric revolution is the wave. We didn't wait to see what it meant. We built the machine. That's what sharks do."

Bryan Jaworski, Founder

SNG was founded in the middle of the wave. Not after it passed. Not before it arrived. In it. The decision to build an electric supermoto wasn't a hedge against a future that might come. It was a clear-eyed recognition that the future was already here and nobody had built the right machine for it.

That's the first meaning of Shark Not Goldfish: thriving during waves of change rather than panicking or waiting them out.

There's a second meaning, and it's the one that gets into why the tagline is "Leave the School Behind."

Fish travel in schools because safety lives in the group. Collective behavior, collective direction, collective risk management. Most markets work like a school — companies follow each other, copy each other's decisions, compete on the same specs for the same customer in the same price band. The school of electric supermoto builders is competing for the 5'10" buyer, the mass-market specification, the lowest-common-denominator ergonomics. They're moving together in the same direction because that's where the most fish are.

SNG left the school behind. Built for the rider nobody else built for. Chose components the community consensus didn't choose because the data said something different. Filed a patent on a battery access system that no production motorcycle has attempted. Sourced a $50 salvaged gas tank and turned it into an engineering solution that looks like OEM work. The school would never have done any of that. The school doesn't do that kind of thing. The school follows the school.

SNG4LIFE

Bryan has "SNG4LIFE" tattooed on his knuckles. The shark fin logo is tattooed on both hands. This isn't a brand for a business plan. This is a life position. The philosophy isn't a marketing choice — it's the founding principle of every build decision, every component selection, every engineering call.

The name comes from that. Shark Not Goldfish is the refusal to follow the school. It's the recognition that in a market full of companies making the same compromises, the differentiated position belongs to whoever builds something different and builds it well. Leave the school behind doesn't mean leave the market behind — it means leave the consensus behavior that produces mediocre machines for mediocre buyers.

SNG machines are built for the rider who already left the school. The KTM buyer. The Ducati buyer. The person who has owned serious motorcycles, who has opinions about suspension and geometry and component selection, who knows the difference between a machine that was engineered and a machine that was assembled. That rider doesn't want to be in the school. They never wanted to be in the school. They want the machine that was built for them.

That's the machine SNG builds.

It is lighter than machines with less performance. It is more capable than machines that weigh more. The industry doesn't have a name for this class yet. We're calling it Ultra Light.

The premium pricing on an SNG machine is not arbitrary. What's Next

Unit 001 is built. The R&D is done. The production run is the next wave. Here's where SNG goes from here.

Unit 001 exists. That fact matters more than any spec sheet or business projection. There is a physical machine — 202 pounds, 39-inch seat height, WP forks, BAC8000 controller, patent-pending clamshell system, gas tank battery enclosure — sitting in Florida that was built from scratch over fourteen months by one builder with the right knowledge and the refusal to stop until it was right.

The R&D is done. The engineering decisions are made. The mistakes have been paid for in time and money and then turned into competitive advantage. What comes next is taking everything that was learned across three prototypes and moving it into production.

The Production Run

The 30-unit Megalodon run is the first step. Thirty numbered gold bikes that carry the same DNA as Unit 001, built to the same specification, with the same component stack and the same hand-formed bodywork. The production build process is not a factory line — it's a documented, repeatable assembly sequence for a small batch of machines that each require the same attention to detail that went into the prototype. The Holochrome Edition and the 520E platform follow. "The prototype proved the machine. The production run proves the brand. These are not the same challenge — and both matter."

Bryan Jaworski, Founder

Sponsor Partnerships

Component supplier relationships are a strategic priority for the next phase. The brands already on the SNG machine — WP Suspension, Lewis Brakes, Warp 9, UFO Plast — represent the kind of component partnerships that serious manufacturers carry. Formalizing those relationships as the production run begins means better pricing, better availability, and the kind of co-branding that reinforces the premium specification story to the buyer who cares about what's on their motorcycle.

Florida's motorsport community is also a natural sponsorship channel. The machine exists in a state with year-round riding, strong motorcycle culture, and a growing concentration of premium buyers. Track days, demo events, and presence at the gatherings where the SNG buyer already spends their time are the near-term marketing strategy. The machine sells itself when people see it in person — particularly the Holochrome. The job is to put it in front of the right people.

The Next Evolution of the Platform

The engineering roadmap is already clear. The 160mm swingarm currently uses stacked extenders — 100mm plus 60mm — to achieve the final geometry. The next generation will replace that with a single billet custom swingarm machined as one piece. It's lighter. It's cleaner. It's a more direct expression of the geometry without the extender stack. That evolution is already designed in principle. It goes into production when the first run proves out the full platform.

The clamshell system is patent-pending. As the patent matures, the licensing opportunity opens. No production motorcycle manufacturer has this system. There is a real possibility that what was designed as a single-bike engineering solution becomes an IP asset with broader market applications.

Every SNG machine that exists in the world carries the story of three prototypes, $31,000, and 1,000 hours. That story is baked into every numbered unit. It's why the machines are worth what they're worth. It's why the buyers who understand the market will understand the value immediately.
