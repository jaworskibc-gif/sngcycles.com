Proof It's Real

Megladon ownership package — leather jacket · Megalodon tee · SNG snapback · keychain   |   Great White Hoodie — "Leave the School Behind"

Two finished, running, photographed bikes exist today. The gold 270E Holochrome and the shark Megaladon Supermoto were hand-built in Florida over 14+ months of R&D — three prototypes, parts bought and resold, every mistake documented. This isn't a concept. The bikes are real.

The founder has already put $31,000 and 1,000 hours into proving this works. Build cost, quality, and process are proven — not estimated. The ebook "How to Build a Savage eBike with AI" documents the full methodology including two complete builds step-by-step. The team is standing by: 2 mechanics, 1 body/wrap specialist.

The body panels were built, not just installed. The fairings start as UFO plastics — Italian-made ABS panels — then the founder cuts them down, heat-guns the curves, and shapes them by hand with a razor blade until they fit the frame perfectly. No mold required, no outside vendor. The shape on that bike exists because someone stood in a garage and made it happen.

Every engineering decision on this bike started with function. The founder rides in the dunes. That means a battery cover that fills with sand is not a battery cover — it's a problem. The extended battery enclosures on competing builds pack with debris, rattle loose, and look like an afterthought because they are one. So the founder bought a gas tank off a totaled bike for $50, cut the bottom off, heat-formed it, used a baking soda plaster to set the shape, and mounted the original tank lid to the frame with a cutout at center. The result: a sealed, factory-looking battery enclosure with built-in plastic mounting points — indistinguishable from a motorcycle gas tank because it is one.

Nobody else is doing this. It wasn't a design decision. It was the obvious solution to a real problem — and "obvious to me" is what $31,000 and 1,000 hours of domain knowledge buys. The aesthetics followed the function. They usually do when the engineer actually rides the bike.

The same principle drives the quick-change system: a custom-fabricated upper subframe, a hidden car-style hood release tucked under the rear fairing, and homemade swivel brackets that hold the plastics at the correct spread while bracing against wind load at speed. Pull the release — you hear the pop — the seat, tank shell, and front fairings lift as one clamshell piece. Battery out in seconds. No tools. No bolts. No sand traps.

That system is the subject of a provisional patent filing. No production motorcycle on earth does this.
